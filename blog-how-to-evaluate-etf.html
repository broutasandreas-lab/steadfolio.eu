How to Evaluate an ETF: 7 Things to Check Before You Invest

5 min read - Educational content, not financial advice

Searching for an ETF can become overwhelming very quickly. Two funds can have similar names, track similar markets, and still differ in cost, concentration, structure, liquidity, or tax treatment.

The quick ETF evaluation checklist: check the index, holdings, costs, tracking difference, size/liquidity, domicile, and fund structure. You do not need to predict which ETF will perform best. You need to understand what you are actually buying.

1. Start with the index or investment objective

Before looking at fees or performance, ask what the ETF is designed to track. An ETF is only a wrapper. The real investment exposure comes from the index, assets, or strategy inside it.

  • What market or asset class does it track?
  • Is the index broad or narrowly focused?
  • Is it market-cap weighted, equal weighted, factor-based, thematic, or actively managed?
  • Does that exposure actually match what you are trying to own?

Two ETFs can both contain large US companies but follow different rules and end up with meaningfully different portfolios.

2. Look inside the holdings — not just the ETF name

Names can sound diversified while the portfolio is heavily concentrated in a few companies, sectors, or countries. Check the number of holdings, the largest positions, and how much of the fund sits in its top 10.

If one sector represents a large part of the fund, ask whether that concentration is intentional. This is where understanding what diversification actually means becomes useful.

3. Compare the total cost

The annual fund fee — often shown as an expense ratio, TER, or ongoing charge depending on the market — matters because it is paid year after year. But it should be compared with funds offering similar exposure.

A cheaper ETF is not automatically better if it tracks a different index or gives you a portfolio you did not want.

Useful rule: first compare the exposure, then compare the cost of getting that exposure.

4. Check the tracking difference

If an ETF aims to follow an index, its return will rarely match the index perfectly. Fees, withholding taxes, trading costs and the way the portfolio is managed can create a gap.

Tracking difference shows how the fund actually performed relative to its benchmark over time. When comparing two ETFs tracking the same index, this can tell you more than looking at the headline fee alone.

If you are not yet clear on the relationship between an index and a fund, read What Is an Index Fund?.

5. Check fund size, liquidity and the bid-ask spread

Fund size and trading activity can affect how easy an ETF is to buy and sell. Larger, established funds often have deeper liquidity, although trading volume alone is not the whole story because ETF liquidity also depends on the underlying assets.

Also look at the bid-ask spread: the difference between the highest price a buyer is offering and the lowest price a seller will accept. Wider spreads can make trading more expensive, particularly in smaller or less liquid products.

6. For European investors, check domicile and UCITS status

For investors in Europe, the fund's legal structure matters. Many retail investors use UCITS ETFs, and the fund's domicile can affect how dividends received by the fund are taxed.

This does not mean one domicile is automatically best for everyone. It means domicile belongs on the checklist rather than being ignored. Our guide to ETF domicile and withholding tax explains the issue in more detail.

7. Check how the ETF handles income and replicates the index

An ETF may be accumulating, meaning income is reinvested inside the fund, or distributing, meaning income is paid out to investors. Neither is universally better; the difference affects how cash flows are handled.

You should also check whether the ETF physically holds the underlying securities or uses another replication method. Understanding the structure helps you compare like with like.

See Accumulating vs Distributing ETFs for the income side of this decision.

A simple ETF evaluation example

Imagine two ETFs both claim to give you broad global equity exposure:

  • ETF A has a lower fee, but its index excludes some markets you expected to own.
  • ETF B costs slightly more, but tracks the broader index you actually wanted and has shown a small, consistent tracking difference.

The correct conclusion is not automatically “buy A” or “buy B.” The point is that the lowest fee is only one part of the evaluation. Exposure comes first.

What should you check first?

  1. What does the ETF track?
  2. What does it actually hold?
  3. How concentrated is it?
  4. What does it cost?
  5. How closely has it tracked its benchmark?
  6. Is it large and liquid enough for your needs?
  7. What is its domicile and structure?

The SteadFolio 7-Point ETF Checklist

If you want a simple way to evaluate an ETF, check these seven things before you invest:

  1. Index and objective — What exactly is the ETF trying to track or achieve?
  2. Holdings — What companies, sectors, countries, or assets are actually inside it?
  3. Total cost — Check the ongoing fund fee, but also consider brokerage costs and spreads.
  4. Tracking difference — How closely has the ETF followed its benchmark after costs?
  5. Fund size and liquidity — Larger, more actively traded funds often have tighter spreads and lower closure risk.
  6. Domicile and regulatory structure — Especially in Europe, check whether the ETF is UCITS-compliant and where it is domiciled.
  7. Distribution and replication — Is it accumulating or distributing? Does it physically hold the assets or use synthetic replication?
SteadFolio principle: Do not ask “Is this ETF good?” before asking “Do I understand what this ETF actually owns, costs, tracks, and does?”

The point is not that ETF A is automatically “better.” The point is that these checks make the trade-offs visible. A fund can be cheap but concentrated, diversified but tax-inefficient for your situation, or popular while still being a poor fit for your goal.

Quick answer: how do you evaluate an ETF?

Start with what it tracks, then check what it owns, what it costs, how closely it follows its benchmark, whether it is liquid enough, where it is domiciled, and how it handles distributions and replication. If you can explain those seven points in plain language, you are already doing more due diligence than simply choosing a fund because it is popular.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you evaluate an ETF?

Start with the index or strategy, then check holdings and concentration, total cost, tracking difference, fund size and liquidity, domicile, and whether the fund is accumulating or distributing. The goal is to understand the exposure and structure before comparing performance.

What makes an ETF good?

There is no universally “good” ETF. A useful ETF is one whose underlying exposure, costs, structure, liquidity and risks match the objective you are trying to achieve.

What is a good expense ratio for an ETF?

Broad passive ETFs often have relatively low annual fees, while niche, thematic or actively managed ETFs may cost more. Compare funds offering similar exposure rather than applying one fee threshold to every ETF.

What does tracking difference mean?

Tracking difference is the gap between an ETF's actual return and the return of the index it aims to follow. Fees, taxes and implementation can create that gap.

How many ETFs do I need?

There is no required number. One broad ETF can already hold hundreds or thousands of securities, while several overlapping funds may add complexity without much extra diversification.
