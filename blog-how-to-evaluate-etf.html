How to Evaluate an ETF Before You Invest

2 min read - Educational content, not financial advice

ETFs (exchange-traded funds) are often recommended as a beginner-friendly way to invest, and for good reason - a single ETF can give you exposure to hundreds of companies at once. But "it's an ETF" doesn't automatically mean "it's a good choice for you." Here's what's actually worth checking before you invest in one.

ETF Evaluation Checklist

Before putting money into any ETF, run it through these five checks:

  • Holdings - what companies, sectors, or countries does it actually contain?
  • Expense ratio - what's the annual fee, and is it justified?
  • Diversification - how many holdings, and how concentrated is the top 10?
  • Fund size and age - is it established enough to be liquid and stable?
  • Tracking difference - does it actually deliver the index's return, after fees?

1. What does it actually hold?

Every ETF tracks something - an index, a sector, a theme. Before anything else, look at the fund's actual holdings. A fund called something broad-sounding might be more concentrated in a handful of companies or a single sector than the name suggests. This information is usually published by the fund provider and updated regularly.

2. Expense Ratios & Liquidity

The expense ratio is the annual fee the fund charges, expressed as a percentage of your investment. It might look tiny - 0.07% versus 0.75%, for example - but over decades, that difference compounds into a meaningful amount of money. As a rough frame of reference, broad index-tracking ETFs often charge well under 0.5%, while more niche or actively managed funds tend to charge more.

Liquidity - how easily you can buy or sell shares without moving the price - tends to go hand in hand with fund size and trading volume. Larger, more established funds are generally easier to trade at a fair price than small or newly launched ones.

Worth remembering: A higher fee doesn't automatically mean better performance. Fees are a certainty; outperformance is not.

3. How diversified is it, really?

Diversification is one of the main reasons people choose ETFs in the first place, but not all ETFs are equally diversified. Some spread your money across thousands of companies globally. Others might be concentrated in one country, one sector, or even a small handful of large companies. Check how many holdings the fund has and how much of it is concentrated in the top 10 positions.

If you want a deeper look at why spreading investments matters and what it can and can't protect you from, our guide on what diversification actually means covers it in more detail.

3 questions worth asking

  • How many total holdings does the fund have?
  • What percentage of the fund is in its top 10 holdings?
  • Is it spread across countries/sectors, or concentrated in one?

4. How long has it existed, and how big is it?

Older, larger funds tend to have more trading history to evaluate and are generally more liquid, meaning it's easier to buy and sell shares without affecting the price much. Very new or very small funds can carry additional risks, including the possibility of the fund closing down if it doesn't attract enough investors.

5. What's the tracking difference?

An index fund is supposed to track its underlying index closely. "Tracking difference" measures how closely it actually does, over time, after fees. A fund that consistently lags its index by more than its stated fee might be worth a second look. If you're not yet sure what an index fund is or why it's structured this way, our guide on what an index fund is and why people recommend them is a good starting point.

A note for UK and US investors

The five factors above apply everywhere, but where you can access an ETF varies. In the UK, most ETFs held in an ISA or general investment account need to be UCITS-compliant, and fund pages typically show a TER (total expense ratio) rather than a US-style expense ratio. In the US, broadly diversified options tracking the S&P 500 or total market indices from providers like Vanguard are common starting points, with expense ratios often quoted separately from any brokerage fees. Either way, the same checklist - holdings, fees, diversification, size, and tracking difference - still applies.

If you're investing from an EU country, two more things are worth understanding before you compare specific funds: what UCITS actually means for which ETFs you can even buy, and how a fund's domicile affects the withholding tax on the dividends it receives. It's also worth knowing whether a fund is accumulating or distributing before you buy, since it changes what happens to any dividends automatically.

Putting it together

None of these five factors need to be perfect on their own - the goal is simply to understand what you're actually buying, rather than choosing something because it was mentioned in a video or a group chat. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good expense ratio for an ETF?

Broad, index-tracking ETFs typically charge well under 0.5% per year, with many popular funds charging 0.03%-0.20%. Niche, thematic, or actively managed ETFs tend to charge more, sometimes 0.5% or higher. Lower fees aren't automatically better, but a higher fee should come with a clear reason.

How many ETFs should I hold?

There's no fixed number - it depends on how diversified each fund already is. A single broad global or total-market ETF can already provide exposure to thousands of companies, so many beginner investors hold just one to three funds rather than trying to piece together diversification from many overlapping ones.

What does tracking difference mean?

Tracking difference measures how closely an ETF's actual return matches the return of the index it's supposed to follow, after fees. A small, consistent gap is normal; a fund that regularly lags its index by more than its stated expense ratio may be worth a second look.

Is a bigger ETF always safer?

Larger, older funds tend to be more liquid and have a longer track record to evaluate, which reduces certain risks like fund closure. But size alone doesn't guarantee a fund fits your goals - what it holds and what it costs still matter more than its size.
