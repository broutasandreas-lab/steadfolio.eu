Understand ETFs

What Is an Index Fund? A Plain-English Guide for Beginners

By The SteadFolio Team · Last reviewed: October 6, 2026 · 9 min read · Educational content, not financial advice

An index fund is an investment fund that tries to copy the performance of a market index, such as the MSCI World or the S&P 500, instead of trying to beat it. It does this by holding the same securities as the index, in roughly the same proportions, and following the index's published rules for what goes in and what comes out.

That simple design is why index funds tend to be cheap, broadly diversified and easy to understand. It is also why they can never do better than the market they track, and will fall with it when it falls. This guide explains how they work, how they differ from ETFs and actively managed funds, what they really cost, and what investors in Europe should check.

In short: an index fund follows a rulebook (the index) rather than a manager's opinions. You get the market's return, minus costs, including the bad years. Whether that suits you depends on your goals, time horizon and how you would react to a large fall.

How an index fund works

An index is a list of securities chosen by a fixed set of rules. The MSCI World Index, for example, covers large and mid-sized companies across 23 developed countries. The S&P 500 covers 500 leading US companies. The index provider decides the rules; the fund simply follows them.

Most popular indices are market-cap weighted: the bigger a company's total market value, the bigger its share of the index. If one company makes up 5% of the index, it will make up about 5% of the fund. When the index provider adds or removes a company, the fund adjusts too. Nobody at the fund is deciding which companies look attractive.

Funds copy their index in one of two broad ways:

  • Full replication: the fund buys every security in the index.
  • Sampling (optimized replication): for very large indices, the fund holds a representative subset designed to behave like the whole index.

Some ETFs use synthetic replication instead, getting the index return through a swap agreement with a bank rather than holding the shares directly. The ETF evaluation checklist explains how to spot which method a fund uses.

A simple example

Suppose you put €100 into a fund tracking a world index with more than a thousand companies. You don't buy one company; you buy a tiny slice of every company in the index, weighted by size. If one company collapses, it is a small fraction of your €100. But if the whole market falls 20%, your fund falls by roughly the same amount, because it owns the whole market.

Index fund vs ETF: what's the difference?

People often use the two terms interchangeably, but they describe different things. "Index fund" describes the strategy (tracking an index). "ETF" describes the wrapper (a fund whose shares trade on a stock exchange). Many ETFs are index funds, but some ETFs are actively managed, and some index funds are traditional mutual funds that don't trade on an exchange at all.

For many retail investors in Europe, the most accessible way to buy an index fund today is a UCITS ETF held at a broker, often through an automatic savings plan. Index mutual funds exist too, but which ones you can buy depends on your country, bank and platform. Neither wrapper is automatically better: compare the total cost of the specific funds and accounts actually available to you.

Index funds vs actively managed funds

An active fund pays a manager and analysts to pick investments, aiming to beat a benchmark. That research costs money, so active funds usually charge more. Higher fees are a head start the manager has to overcome every single year.

S&P Dow Jones Indices tracks how this plays out in its SPIVA Europe scorecards. Report after report has found that most actively managed equity funds domiciled in Europe trailed their benchmark over ten-year periods. That doesn't mean no active fund ever wins. It means it is hard to identify the winners in advance, and paying more does not reliably buy better results.

  • Index (passive) fund: aims to match an index, usually at lower cost, with no manager judgement about which companies to own.
  • Active fund: aims to beat a benchmark through selection, usually at higher cost, with results that depend on the manager.

What does an index fund cost?

The headline number is the ongoing charge, often shown as the TER (total expense ratio). It is a percentage of your investment taken each year inside the fund, so you never see a separate bill. You'll find it in the fund's factsheet and in its Key Information Document (KID), which EU rules require for retail investment products (PRIIPs Regulation).

The TER isn't the whole cost. Also check:

  • Broker or platform fees: per-order fees, account fees or savings-plan fees.
  • Bid-ask spread: the gap between buying and selling prices on the exchange.
  • Currency conversion: if the fund trades in a currency other than your account's.
  • Tracking difference: how far the fund's actual return lagged (or beat) its index after all internal costs.

Why small fee differences matter

On €10,000, a 0.20% yearly charge is about €20 a year; a 1.50% charge is about €150. The gap grows over time because fees are taken every year from a growing balance.

Hypothetical illustration: €200 invested every month for 25 years (€60,000 in total), assuming a constant 6% yearly return before fees. With a 0.20% yearly fee, the simplified result is roughly €134,400. With a 1.50% fee it is roughly €110,600, about €23,800 less. Real returns are never constant, and this ignores taxes, trading costs and inflation. It shows the effect of fees, not a forecast.

Cost matters, but only after you've confirmed two funds give you the exposure you want. A cheaper fund tracking a narrower or different index is not automatically the better choice.

Diversification: what it does and doesn't do

Owning hundreds or thousands of companies means no single company's failure can sink your investment. That is the main practical benefit of index investing, and why it is a common starting point. (See What Is Diversification? for the wider picture.)

But diversification inside one index has limits:

  • It doesn't protect you from the whole market falling. In a broad market crash, a broad index fund falls too.
  • Market-cap weighting concentrates. The largest companies, sectors and countries get the biggest weights. A "world" fund can be heavily tilted towards one country or a handful of large companies. Check the factsheet's top-10 holdings and country breakdown.
  • The index defines the limits. A developed-markets index leaves out emerging markets; an S&P 500 fund holds only US companies; a thematic index can hold just a few dozen stocks in one industry.

The risks of index funds

  • Market risk: you receive the market's losses as well as its gains, and there is no manager trying to sidestep a downturn. Prices can fall sharply and stay down for years. Read what happens during a stock market crash.
  • Concentration risk: as above, cap-weighted indices can lean heavily on a few names or one country.
  • Currency risk: a euro-based investor holding a fund of US or global companies is also exposed to exchange-rate moves, even if the fund itself is priced in euros.
  • Tracking risk: the fund's return can drift from the index because of costs, taxes and how it replicates.
  • Behavior risk: for many people the biggest risk is selling in a panic during a fall. A plan you can stick with matters more than picking the perfect fund. See why behavior matters more than strategy.

Index funds in Europe: UCITS, domicile and income

If you invest from the EU, a few European specifics affect which index funds you can buy and how they work:

  • UCITS: most index ETFs sold to EU retail investors are UCITS funds, an EU framework with rules on diversification, custody of assets and investor information (UCITS Directive). Read What Are UCITS ETFs? for what the label does and doesn't guarantee.
  • KID availability: because EU brokers must provide a KID to retail clients, many US-domiciled ETFs are generally not offered to EU retail investors. Most Europeans therefore use European-domiciled versions of the same indices.
  • Domicile and withholding tax: where a fund is based can affect how much dividend tax is lost before income reaches the fund. See ETF domicile and withholding tax.
  • Accumulating vs distributing: some share classes reinvest dividends automatically, others pay them out. Neither is universally better. See Accumulating vs Distributing ETFs.
  • Your own taxes: how gains and dividends are taxed depends on your country of tax residence. Check local rules or a qualified tax adviser.

How to start with index funds, step by step

  1. Make sure you're ready. Essentials covered, an emergency buffer in place, expensive debt under control. See should you build an emergency fund first?
  2. Decide what market you want to own. A broad world index, a single region, or a mix with bonds. Asset allocation explains the trade-offs.
  3. Compare specific funds that track it, using the 7-point ETF checklist.
  4. Choose where to hold it. Compare broker fees, savings plans and currency costs in the broker comparison.
  5. Automate a regular amount. Dollar-cost averaging removes the pressure of picking the right day.
  6. Leave it alone. Review once or twice a year, not every day.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an index fund the same as an ETF?

Not exactly. "Index fund" describes the strategy of tracking an index; "ETF" describes a fund that trades on a stock exchange. Many ETFs are index funds, but some ETFs are actively managed, and some index funds are traditional mutual funds that don't trade on an exchange.

Are index funds safe?

No investment in shares is safe in the sense of guaranteed. A broad index fund removes the risk of one company sinking your investment, but it still falls when the overall market falls, sometimes sharply and for years. It suits money you can leave invested for a long time.

How much does an index fund cost?

The main cost is the yearly ongoing charge (TER), shown in the factsheet and the Key Information Document. Broad index funds are usually among the cheapest funds available. Also count broker fees, the bid-ask spread and any currency conversion costs.

Can I buy index funds in Europe?

Yes. Most EU retail investors access index investing through UCITS ETFs bought via a broker, often with an automatic savings plan. Index mutual funds are also available through some banks and platforms, depending on your country.

Do index funds pay dividends?

If the companies in the index pay dividends, the fund receives them. A distributing share class pays them out to you; an accumulating share class reinvests them inside the fund. Check the fund name or factsheet for "Acc" or "Dist".

Can an index fund beat the market?

By design, no. It aims to match its index, and after costs it will usually trail the index slightly. The argument for index funds is that many actively managed funds also fail to beat the market after their higher fees, and it's hard to know in advance which ones will.
