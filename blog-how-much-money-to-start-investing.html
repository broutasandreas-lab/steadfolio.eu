How Much Money Do You Actually Need to Start Investing?

2 min read - Educational content, not financial advice

You can start investing with as little as €10 or £10 thanks to fractional shares, which let you buy a portion of a share instead of a whole one. This is one of the most common reasons people delay starting: the assumption that investing requires thousands of euros sitting around first. In most cases today, that's simply outdated.

The short answer

Many brokers today allow you to start with very small amounts - sometimes as little as a few euros, especially with fractional shares. The barrier that used to exist technically doesn't really apply anymore for most beginner investors.

Why the old advice doesn't fit anymore

Older investing advice often assumed you needed enough money to buy whole shares of expensive companies, or that trading fees would eat up small investments. Fractional shares and the rise of low-fee or fee-free brokers have largely changed that math for everyday investors.

Worth remembering: Availability of fractional shares and specific fee structures vary by broker and by country - it's worth checking the specifics of any platform you're considering.

Why starting small is actually a feature, not a limitation

Starting with an amount that genuinely doesn't stress you out - even if it feels "too small to matter" - lets you build the habit and emotional comfort of investing before larger amounts are involved. This connects directly to the idea of dollar-cost averaging: consistency matters more than the size of any single contribution, especially early on.

What matters more than the starting amount

  • Consistency - investing something regularly beats waiting to invest a "big enough" amount once.
  • Only investing money you won't need soon - money for near-term expenses or emergencies generally shouldn't be tied up in investments that can go down in value. If you haven't set that aside yet, our guide on building an emergency fund before investing is worth reading first.
  • Understanding what you're buying - the amount matters far less than whether you actually understand the investment itself.

A note for UK and US investors

In the UK, platforms like Trading 212 and Freetrade offer fractional shares with no account minimum, and many let you invest inside a Stocks and Shares ISA, which shields gains from UK tax. In the US, brokers such as Fidelity, Schwab, and Robinhood offer fractional shares with $0 minimums, often inside a standard brokerage account or an IRA for retirement-focused investing. The specific platform matters less than the underlying point: small, regular amounts are genuinely enough to begin with in both markets.

A more useful question

Instead of "how much do I need to start," a more useful question is often "how much can I comfortably set aside regularly, without it affecting my day-to-day life?" That number is different for everyone, and it's allowed to be small.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much money do I need to start investing?

You can start investing with as little as €10 or £10 thanks to fractional shares, which let you buy a portion of a share instead of a whole one. Many brokers today have no account minimums, so the barrier that used to exist technically doesn't apply anymore for most beginner investors.

Is it worth investing small amounts?

Yes. Starting with a small, comfortable amount lets you build the habit and emotional comfort of investing before larger amounts are involved. Consistency over time generally matters more than the size of any single contribution, especially early on.

What are fractional shares?

Fractional shares let you buy a portion of a single share rather than a whole one, so you can invest a fixed euro or dollar amount - for example €20 - in a company or fund even if one full share costs much more than that.
