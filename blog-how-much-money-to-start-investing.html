How Much Money Do You Actually Need to Start Investing?

4 min read - Educational content, not financial advice

You probably do not need thousands of euros to start investing. Depending on your broker and what you want to buy, you may be able to start with a very small amount.

The short answer: there is no universal minimum. €10, €50 or €100 may be technically enough on some platforms. The more important question is whether the amount fits your finances and whether you can invest it consistently.

Can you really start with €10?

Sometimes, yes — but not everywhere. Some brokers offer fractional shares, which allow you to buy part of a share or ETF instead of a whole unit. Other brokers or securities may require whole-share purchases.

So instead of assuming that €10 is always enough, check:

  • whether your broker has an account minimum;
  • whether the investment can be bought fractionally;
  • whether there are trading, currency-conversion, custody, or other fees;
  • whether a very small trade makes sense after those costs.

Is €50 enough to start investing?

It can be. €50 will not create a dramatic return overnight, but that is not the useful test.

If €50 fits your budget, a small contribution can help you learn what happens when you place an order, how market movements feel, and whether you can follow a process without constantly changing it.

We break down the numbers separately in Is €50 a Month Really Worth Investing?.

What about €100 or €250 a month?

The principle is the same. A larger contribution can build capital faster, but it should not come at the cost of your short-term financial stability.

Monthly amount What it can be useful for
€10–€25Learning the mechanics and building a first habit where fractional investing is available.
€50A realistic starting contribution for some beginners without needing a large lump sum.
€100More flexibility, depending on ETF/share prices and broker features.
€250+Potentially faster capital accumulation — but only if the contribution remains sustainable.

These are not recommendations. They are examples of why the “minimum amount” question is different from the “right amount for me” question.

What should come before investing?

Starting small does not mean investing should always be your first use of spare cash.

Money for rent, bills, near-term goals or unexpected expenses generally should not depend on what the market happens to do next month. High-interest debt may also deserve attention before taking investment risk.

If you do not yet have a financial buffer, read Why You Need an Emergency Fund Before You Start Investing.

Minimum to start vs amount worth investing

This distinction matters:

  • Minimum to start is a platform question: what is the smallest order your broker allows?
  • Amount worth investing is a personal finance question: what can you sustainably put aside while accepting market risk?

Those are not the same thing.

Why waiting for a “big enough” amount can backfire

Some beginners postpone investing until they have €1,000, €5,000 or more because a small first contribution feels pointless.

But waiting for the amount to feel impressive can turn into permanent delay. If your financial foundations are already in place, learning with a manageable amount can be more useful than waiting for a psychologically satisfying number.

This is closely related to dollar-cost averaging: using a regular contribution process instead of repeatedly deciding when the “perfect” moment has arrived.

Fractional shares: useful, but not universal

Fractional shares have reduced one historical barrier to investing. If one full share or ETF unit costs more than your monthly contribution, fractional investing can allow you to invest a fixed cash amount instead.

But availability varies by broker, country and security. Always check the actual platform terms rather than assuming every stock or ETF can be purchased fractionally.

A better question than “How much do I need?”

Ask: “How much can I invest regularly without needing that money back at the wrong time?”

That question forces you to think about cash flow, emergency savings, time horizon and risk — the things that matter more than whether your first investment is €20 or €2,000.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much money do I need to start investing?

There is no universal minimum. Some brokers allow very small purchases or fractional shares, while others require whole units. What matters most is that you are not using money needed for bills, emergencies or expensive debt.

Is €50 enough to start investing?

€50 can be enough if your broker supports purchases at that level and the amount fits your finances. It will not produce large returns quickly, but it can be enough to begin learning and building a repeatable process.

Can I start investing with €100?

In many cases, yes. Whether €100 is enough depends on your broker, the asset price, and whether fractional investing is available.

Do I need an emergency fund before investing?

Money needed for unexpected expenses generally should not depend on short-term market performance. Many beginners build an emergency buffer before or alongside long-term investing.

Are fractional shares available everywhere?

No. Availability depends on the broker, country and security. Some platforms offer fractional stocks or ETFs, while others require whole units.
